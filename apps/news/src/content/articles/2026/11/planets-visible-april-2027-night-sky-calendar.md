**The Full Moon falls on Tuesday, Apr. 20, 2027, at 6:27 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time (EDT), and the Lyrid meteor shower peaks the night of Apr. 21-22**, according to the U.S. Naval Observatory (USNO) and the American Meteor Society (AMS). The National Astronomical Observatory of Japan (NAOJ) lists no opposition in April, and Saturn is at conjunction with the Sun on Apr. 7. Print the Full Moon night on one page with the [April 2027 night sky sheet](#downloads).

| Wed., Apr. 28 | Mercury at superior conjunction with the Sun | 5:22 p.m. EDT | NAOJ |

NAOJ publishes in Japan Standard Time (JST), nine hours ahead of Universal Time (UT). We converted every NAOJ time: Mars's "2027-04-03 02:33" JST is 1:33 p.m. EDT on Apr. 2, and Mercury's "2027-04-29 06:22" JST is 5:22 p.m. EDT on Apr. 28. The USNO times are in UT, also converted. Daylight saving time began Sunday, Mar. 14, 2027, under [15 U.S. Code 260a](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/260a), so every April time is in EDT.

## Which planets are visible in April 2027?

The NAOJ table has no April row for a greatest elongation or an opposition. The rows it does have, and the dates around them, put the planets in these places.

| Planet | NAOJ rows | Where it sits in April |
|---|---|---|
| Mars | Opposition Feb. 20; stationary Apr. 2; eastern quadrature May 26 | Evening sky |
| Jupiter | Opposition Feb. 11; stationary Apr. 13; eastern quadrature May 9 | Evening sky |
| Uranus | Eastern quadrature Feb. 21; conjunction with the Sun May 27 | Evening sky, low and fading |
| Saturn | Conjunction with the Sun Apr. 7 | Lost in the Sun's glare |
| Venus | Greatest elongation west Jan. 4; superior conjunction Aug. 12 | Morning sky |
| Mercury | Superior conjunction with the Sun Apr. 28 | Morning sky before Apr. 28, low |
| Neptune | Conjunction with the Sun Mar. 24 | Morning sky after the conjunction |

The "where it sits" column is our reading of the NAOJ rows, not a NAOJ statement. A planet is stationary when it pauses against the stars before reversing course. NAOJ gives dates, not rise and set times or brightness, and the USNO service used here covers only the Sun and Moon, so we do not print planet times. For your city, use a planetarium app. The month before is in the [March 2027 night sky calendar](/news/planets-visible-march-2027-night-sky-calendar/), and the whole year is in the [meteor showers 2027 calendar](/news/meteor-showers-2027-calendar-peak-dates-moon-phase-best-nights/).

## What are the Moon phases in April 2027?

The USNO lists the new Moon on Tuesday, Apr. 6, first quarter on Tuesday, Apr. 13, the Full Moon on Tuesday, Apr. 20 and last quarter on Wednesday, Apr. 28. For every phase of the year, see the [Moon phases 2027 guide](/news/moon-phases-2027-full-moon-calendar/).

In all four cities the Moon rises after sunset on Apr. 20, between 9 minutes later in New York and 15 minutes later in Denver, and sets before sunrise that morning. In New York the exact Full Moon, 6:27 p.m., comes before the 7:50 p.m. moonrise. The printable lists the same Moon times.

## When do the Lyrids peak in 2027?

The AMS calendar lists the Lyrids as active Apr. 14-30, 2027, with a peak the night of Apr. 21-22. The USNO puts the Full Moon at 6:27 p.m. EDT on Apr. 20, so the Moon is one to two days past full at the peak and moonlight hurts the view. The sources used here give no hourly rate, so we print none.

## Is there an eclipse in April 2027?

No. NASA's tables list the annular solar eclipse of Feb. 6, the penumbral lunar eclipse of Feb. 20, the total solar eclipse of Aug. 2 and penumbral lunar eclipses on Jul. 18 and Aug. 17, none in April.

## Where do these dates come from?

The Moon phases are from the [USNO moon phases data](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/api/moon/phases/year?year=2027), labeled in UT. The planet events are from the [NAOJ Koyomi table](https://eco.mtk.nao.ac.jp/cgi-bin/koyomi/cande/phenomena_en.cgi?year=2027). The Sun and Moon times are from the USNO rise, set and transit service. The meteor dates are from the [AMS calendar](https://www.amsmeteors.org/calendar/). The eclipses come from NASA's [solar](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEdecade/SEdecade2021.html) and [lunar](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/LEdecade/LEdecade2021.html) tables.
