**The New York Times (NYT) Crossword for Wednesday, Nov. 11, 2026, was constructed by Damon Gulczynski and edited by Will Shortz: a 15-by-15 grid with 78 clues.** A single revealer explains the theme, four clues carry a wordplay question mark, and the grid is more open than Tuesday's. Hints for every clue come first, each behind a tap, and the answers close the page.

## Four question-mark clues and the tricks behind them

A question mark warns that the plain reading of a clue is a decoy. Today three of the four sit in the Across list and one is Down. No explanation below names an answer.

- **34-Across, "Cried sheepishly?"** (5 letters). Treat "cried" as a verb in the past tense and "sheepishly" as an instruction to cry the way a sheep does. The entry is the sound of a lamb, turned into a past-tense verb with a double vowel.
- **55-Across, "Cries cattily?"** (4 letters). Same device, present tense this time: "cries" is the verb and "cattily" names the animal. The entry is the noise a kitten makes, with an S on the end.
- **61-Across, "Image that's difficult to make out ... as suggested four times in this puzzle?"** (11 letters). This is the revealer. The entry is the name for the kind of picture the theme is about, and the clue's last phrase counts the theme entries: four.
- **4-Down, "How about a quick peek?"** (7 letters). The clue is a spoken question, and the entry is a standard polite way to ask it: three short words run together, the middle one a single letter.

## How today's theme works

Four entries carry the theme: 17-Across and 38-Across, then 10-Down and 25-Down. Their own clues are plain, so nothing on the surface gives them away. The trick sits in the letters: each entry is two words run together, and a short word hides across the join, using the end of the first word and the start of the second. The four hidden words run three to five letters, and each is the subject of a well-known blurry photograph. The revealer at 61-Across then says what that kind of picture is called. The spoiler section below shows each split.

## How the grid is built

The grid is 15 by 15, which is 225 squares: 36 are black and 189 hold a letter. On Tuesday the grid had 40 black squares and 185 letters, so the white space grew by four squares. The black pattern is the same when the grid turns upside down, and the theme respects that: 17-Across at the left of row 3 turns into 61-Across at the right of row 13, and 10-Down in column 13 turns into 25-Down in column 3.

There are 78 entries, 39 Across and 39 Down, against 76 on Tuesday (35 Across, 41 Down). Lengths run from 3 to 11 letters. The tally is 20 entries of 3 letters (10 Across, 10 Down), 26 of 4 (10 and 16), 12 of 5 (10 and 2), six of 6 (two and four), eight of 7 (five and three), two of 9 (both Down) and four of 11 (two and two). Nothing runs 8, 10 or more than 11 letters. Tuesday's longest entry was 15 squares, today's is 11, and there are four of them.

The four 11-square entries form a pinwheel. 17-Across fills columns 1 to 11 of row 3, 10-Down fills rows 1 to 11 of column 13, 61-Across fills columns 5 to 15 of row 13, and 25-Down fills rows 5 to 15 of column 3. The two 9-letter entries, 8-Down and 35-Down, run down columns 9 and 7. The lettered cells are all single letters, so there is no rebus. Two clues point at each other: 16-Across and 19-Across each say the other is an anagram of its own answer. 15-Down is a single asterisk.
