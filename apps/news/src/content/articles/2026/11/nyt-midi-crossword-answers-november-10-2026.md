**The New York Times (NYT) Midi Crossword for Tuesday, Nov. 10, 2026, is titled "Triple Dog Dare" and has 29 clues on a nine-by-nine grid, built by Paolo Pasco.** Ian Livengood is listed as editor. Each clue has a hint that stays shut until you tap it, and the answers wait in a marked section at the bottom.

## What "Triple Dog Dare" asks of the solver

The official data gives a title and a constructor and nothing else about the idea, so this is what the clues themselves say. One Across clue describes a three-headed figure from mythology and asks whether the puzzle's own figures are like it. A "triple dare" is the strongest challenge in the playground sense, and the title seems to borrow that phrase for a puzzle where things come in threes.

The easiest place to test that is the top row, which has two short Across entries separated by black squares. Fill them from their crossing Down entries and compare the letters inside each one. The longer Across entries in the middle and lower rows are worth a second look too.

## The clue with the loosest wordplay

The least literal clue is 6-Down, "This: :" with five letters. The clue is not a definition in words. It prints a small piece of punctuation and uses "This" to point at it, so the clue is its own example. Read it aloud and you get a pointing word followed by the mark itself; the answer is the name of that mark, which is also the name of a body part. It crosses the long entry in row 4 at its fourth letter.

Clues are quoted as printed, so a few carry capital letters for emphasis (NOT), for a publication name (VICE) or for a sound (DUH); none of them is an abbreviation.

## Tuesday's grid next to Monday's

Monday's Midi had 13 black squares and 68 letters across 31 clues. Tuesday's has 17 black squares, 64 letters and 29 clues, so the board is more broken up. The size is the same, nine squares by nine, and the editor's name is the same too, but the constructor changes from Ian Livengood to Paolo Pasco.

The mix shifts toward four-letter words: nine of Tuesday's entries are that long, against three on Monday. The two nine-letter entries are one Across (row 4, the only row with no black square) and one Down (column 6, the full height of the grid). Monday's nine-letter entries were three Across and one Down. Row 6 is mostly black, with only three white squares in the middle, so the top and bottom halves meet through a narrow gap.

The two eight-letter Across entries sit in rows 7 and 8, one above the other, and each starts one square in from the left edge. Because they stack, every letter in one of them sits directly above or below a letter of the other.
