### Why 10 swaps is the floor

Count the tiles first: 16 of the 21 start in the wrong square. A swap that fixes two tiles needs two tiles that each sit where the other belongs. Scanning every pair on this board finds exactly four such pairs, and none shares a tile with another:

- L in row 1, column 4 and N in row 2, column 5
- A in row 2, column 1 and C in row 4, column 5
- E in row 3, column 1 and C in row 3, column 5
- U in row 3, column 2 and A in row 5, column 4

Those four swaps (3, 4, 6 and 7 above) settle 8 tiles. The other 8 tiles cannot trade in pairs, so they form longer loops: one loop of 5 tiles (swaps 1, 2, 8 and 9) and one of 3 tiles (swaps 5 and 10). A loop of n tiles costs n minus 1 swaps, which gives the rule: swaps = misplaced tiles minus number of loops. Here that is 16 minus 6 loops, or 10.

Nine would need 7 loops. With at most four two-tile loops, every other loop holds at least three tiles, and the best split of the 16 tiles tops out at 6 loops; trading a two-tile loop for longer ones never beats it. A search over all shorter routes confirms that none exists.

The tile that travels most in this route is the H, the only one of its letter. It is swapped three times (swaps 1, 2 and 8) before it lands in row 3, column 4. The R in row 2, column 3 and the U in row 3, column 4 are swapped twice each, and the other 13 tiles that move are swapped once.
