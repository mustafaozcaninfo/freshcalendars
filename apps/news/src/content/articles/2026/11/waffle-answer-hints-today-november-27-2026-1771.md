## Sixteen tiles share a letter: why one yellow can have two homes

Count the finished board by letter. Two letters fill three squares each, five letters fill two squares each, and five letters appear once. That is 7 repeating letters covering 16 of the 21 tiles.

The repeats reach every color. All 5 green tiles carry a repeating letter, as do 3 of the 6 yellow tiles and 8 of the 10 gray tiles. Only the 5 single-use letters are free of the problem, and none of them is green.

A yellow tile says its letter belongs somewhere else in one of its words. It does not say where. When the letter appears once on the board, only one square can want it. When it appears two or three times, a second open square may want the same letter, and that square can sit in a crossing word. Three of today's six yellow tiles have two open squares like that, and the other three have exactly one.

So a swap that looks right in one word may spend a tile the crossing word needs. A tile already green is a different case: it is final for its square, even when its letter appears again elsewhere.

### Why 10 is the minimum

Sixteen tiles start out of place, so a route of 10 swaps means the tiles fall into 6 loops (16 minus 6). A computer search over every sequence of swaps between misplaced tiles found nothing at 8 or 9 swaps and found the route above, so 10 is the minimum.
