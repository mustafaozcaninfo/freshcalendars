**The U.S. Government Publishing Office (GPO) style manual writes 12 p.m. as noon and 12 a.m. as midnight, but the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) says 12 a.m. and 12 p.m. are ambiguous and should not be used.** That is from NIST's "Times of Day FAQs" (page updated Oct. 11, 2024) and the GPO Style Manual 2016. Below: a 24-row clock card, how a "midnight" deadline gets misread, and a table of safe ways to write a time. A one-page clock card to print is at the end.

## Is 12 noon a.m. or p.m.?

Neither, according to NIST. The agency says "a.m." and "p.m." abbreviate "ante meridiem" and "post meridiem," which mean "before noon" and "after noon," respectively. In NIST's words: "Since noon is neither before noon nor after noon, a designation of either a.m. or p.m. is incorrect."

The GPO Style Manual, chapter 12 on numerals, rule 12.9b (clock time), takes the everyday convention instead and lists "12 p.m. (12 noon)" and "12 a.m. (12 midnight)." Both are federal sources, and they point different ways, so the safe move is the one NIST suggests: write "noon" and "midnight" as words, as in its example of a business open "from 8 a.m. to noon or weekends from 3:30 p.m. until midnight."

| Source | Noon | Midnight | What it says |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPO Style Manual 2016 | 12 p.m. (12 noon) | 12 a.m. (12 midnight) | The style the manual lists for clock time |
| NIST Times of Day FAQs | "noon," not a.m. or p.m. | "Midnight is both twelve hours before noon and twelve hours after noon" | "12 a.m. and 12 p.m. are ambiguous and should not be used" |

## Is 12 a.m. midnight, and which day does it belong to?

GPO's style says yes, 12 a.m. is midnight. The trouble is the date. NIST says midnight is both 12 hours before noon and 12 hours after noon, so one clock reading can mean the end of one day or the start of the next. NIST describes using "the designation of 0000 to refer to midnight at the beginning of a given day (or date) and 2400 to designate the end of a given day (or date)."

NASA's Goddard page, which hosts Markus Kuhn's summary of the international date and time standard, says the same thing: "As every day both starts and ends with midnight, the two notations 00:00 and 24:00 are available to distinguish the two midnights that can be associated with one date." Its example: 1995-02-04 24:00 is the same instant as 1995-02-05 00:00. It adds that "00:00 is usually the preferred notation for midnight and not 24:00." Our explainer on [what International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 8601 is](/news/what-is-iso-8601/) covers the date format those pages use.

## Does a midnight deadline mean the start or the end of the day?

The word alone does not say, and one agency changed its rule over it. In a final rule published in the Federal Register on Nov. 17, 2023 (88 FR 80091), the Federal Labor Relations Authority (FLRA) changed its electronic filing deadline from midnight Eastern Time on the due date to "11:59 p.m." on the due date, and wrote that "the 11:59 eFiling deadline is 11:59 p.m. Eastern Time." In its Sept. 29, 2023 proposal, the FLRA gave the reason: "because midnight technically starts a new day."

That is one agency's rule, not a general legal test. If a form, contract or notice says "midnight," read its own definition or ask. NIST's advice for what you write yourself: "specification of an event as occurring on a particular day at 11:59 p.m. or 12:01 a.m. is a good idea." The same logic applies to a countdown: the new year begins at 00:00 on the first day of the year. Our [New Year's Eve date page](/when-is/new-years-eve/) has the day, and [the midnight countdown by time zone](/news/new-years-countdown-2027-midnight-every-time-zone/) has the clocks.

## How should you write noon, midnight and deadlines so nobody misreads them?

The table applies NIST's advice and the NASA notation to everyday cases. The "write this" column is our wording of that advice, not a quote.

| Situation | Risky | Write this |
|---|---|---|
| Deadline | "Due midnight" | "11:59 p.m. on the due date" (the FLRA wording) |
| Start of an event just after the date changes | "12 a.m. Friday" | "12:01 a.m. Friday" |
| Store or office hours | "12 p.m. to 8 p.m." | "Noon to 8 p.m." |
| Closing at the end of the day | "Open until 12 a.m." | "Open until midnight" |
| Schedules and timestamps | "12 a.m." | "00:00" for the start of a date, "24:00" for its end, with the date |

Computers avoid the question by stopping short of 24. The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) says in Request for Comments (RFC) 3339, section 5.7, that "this profile of ISO 8601 only allows values between "00" and "23" for the hour in order to reduce confusion." The ISO 8601 standard itself is described by the NASA and IETF pages cited here; our guide to it has the full date forms.
