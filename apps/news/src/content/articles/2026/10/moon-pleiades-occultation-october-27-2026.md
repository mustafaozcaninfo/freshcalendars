**From the eastern United States tonight, Tue., Oct. 27, 2026, the Moon passes in front of the Pleiades star cluster: in New York City it hides Alcyone, the brightest star of the cluster, at 8:26 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) and uncovers it at 8:43 p.m., and in Chicago the star is hidden from 7:21 p.m. to 7:58 p.m. Central Daylight Time (CDT).** The times come from our own calculation, explained below. NASA's October skywatching guide, published Oct. 1, 2026, says to look for the Moon passing close to the cluster on the night of Oct. 27 into Oct. 28, and to look east in the evening as the Moon and cluster climb higher.

## What is happening to the Pleiades tonight?

An occultation is the Moon passing in front of a star and hiding it. The Pleiades, also called the Seven Sisters or Messier 45, sit roughly 445 light-years from Earth in the constellation Taurus, according to NASA's Hubble Messier catalog, and the cluster is easy to see with the unaided eye on a dark night.

The U.S. Naval Observatory (USNO) lists the Full Moon at 04:12 Universal Time (UT) on Oct. 26, 2026, which is 12:12 a.m. EDT that Monday. Tonight's Moon is a waning gibbous, about 95% lit by our calculation (the [Hunter's Moon story](/news/hunters-moon-october-2026-full-moon-time/) has the full-Moon details).

In Pittsburgh, Cleveland and Detroit, Electra disappears with the Moon only about 2° up, and in Minneapolis Merope does so with the Moon about 3° up, so those are the hardest events on the list. ## Where is the covering not visible?

In Miami, Orlando, New Orleans, Houston, Dallas, San Antonio, Denver, Phoenix, Los Angeles and Seattle the covering happens before moonrise or in twilight. In Kansas City the Moon is about 1° up at the time, which is practically not visible. From these places the Moon only passes near the cluster. You can still see the two together later in the evening: NASA's chart shows the Moon next to the Pleiades looking east at around 11 p.m.

## How do I watch the Moon cover the stars?

- **Find a clear, low eastern horizon.** In most eastern cities the Moon sits low in the east-northeast during the event, from 4° up in St. Louis to 20° in Boston.
- **Start 5 minutes early.** Independent predictions can differ by a minute or two.
- **Use binoculars or a small telescope at low power.** The bright Moon washes out the fainter Pleiades stars, and binoculars make the event much easier to follow. We do not expect the vanishing stars to be easy with the unaided eye next to a Moon about 95% lit.
- **Watch the right edge.** The Moon moves east against the stars by about its own width each hour, so stars vanish at its eastern, leading edge and reappear at its western edge. Tonight the Moon is waning, so the leading edge is the slightly shaded side and the stars blink out there. They reappear at the bright edge, which is harder to catch.

Weather decides the rest. ## How did we get these times?

Fresh Calendars calculated them with Astronomy Engine, an open-source ephemeris by Don Cross, comparing the Moon's position as seen from each city with catalog positions of the stars. We rounded to the minute. Predictions from different programs often differ by a minute or two, so start watching early.

NASA does not give city times; its guide describes the Moon passing near the Pleiades. The occultation times are ours, and the sky has the final word.
