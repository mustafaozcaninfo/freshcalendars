This is an astronomy story. Retrograde describes where Mercury appears against the stars, and nothing more here.

## What does Mercury retrograde mean?

Mercury retrograde is an apparent backward drift of Mercury, westward against the background stars as seen from Earth. NASA's "Mercury: Facts" page says Mercury "speeds around the Sun every 88 days, traveling through space at nearly 29 miles (47 kilometers) per second, faster than any other planet." Because Mercury circles the Sun faster than Earth does, it periodically catches up with Earth and passes between Earth and the Sun. During that pass, our line of sight makes Mercury seem to reverse direction for a few weeks. It never stops orbiting the Sun in the same direction.

A "station" is the moment the apparent motion changes from eastward to westward (retrograde station) or back (direct station). Our times are those stations.

## Is Mercury in retrograde right now?

Yes. As of Thu., Oct. 29, 2026, Mercury is between its Oct. 24 and Nov. 13 stations. In that time its ecliptic longitude, the position measured along the Sun's path through the sky, goes from about 231.0 degrees back to about 215.0 degrees. That is a drift of about 16 degrees, roughly 32 full-Moon widths, since the full Moon is about half a degree wide.

## How were these times calculated?

We used the NASA JPL [Horizons System](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/): an observer table for Mercury (target 199) from Earth's center, with observer ecliptic longitude at 1-hour steps, computed by Fresh Calendars on Oct. 29, 2026. A station is the hour in which the daily change in ecliptic longitude switches sign. Times are rounded to the hour, so published almanac times can differ from ours by an hour or so. The Mercury figures on speed come from NASA's [Mercury: Facts](https://science.nasa.gov/mercury/facts/) page.
