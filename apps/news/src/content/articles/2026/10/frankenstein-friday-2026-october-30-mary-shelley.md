**Frankenstein Friday falls on Friday, Oct. 30, 2026, the last Friday of October and the day before Halloween.** Libraries and museums use the day for Mary Shelley's 1818 novel *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*. The Moraine Valley Community College Library in Illinois describes it as "a day devoted to Mary Shelley's iconic novel." No founder is documented; some calendar sites name one, but we found no primary record, so we leave the name out. Below: how the book began, what it says that the films changed, where to read it free, and a printable reading sheet.

## How did Mary Shelley come to write Frankenstein?

It began with a dare. The U.S. National Library of Medicine (NLM) tells the story in its exhibition *Frankenstein: Penetrating the Secrets of Nature*. In the summer of 1816, Mary Godwin and the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley, whom she married later that year, stayed near Lake Geneva in Switzerland and visited Lord Byron, who was living at the Villa Diodati with his physician, John William Polidori. "One night, Byron suggested that they take part in a competition to write a terrifying tale."

Mary was "only 18 years old when she was inspired by a 'waking dream'" in which she saw "the hideous phantasm of a man stretched out" that began to "show signs of life," the NLM says. She was born Aug. 30, 1797, the daughter of the writers William Godwin and Mary Wollstonecraft, who died that September after giving birth to her.

The novel came out in London in 1818, printed for Lackington, Hughes, Harding, Mavor and Jones in three volumes, as the 1818 title page on Project Gutenberg shows. It "appeared anonymously," the NLM says, and the first volume is dedicated to William Godwin by "the author." For the 1831 edition, Shelley "wrote a new preface and made several changes in the text."

## Is Frankenstein the name of the monster?

No. Victor Frankenstein is the young scientist who builds a living being and then abandons it. "Mary Shelley had not given her monster a name," the NLM says; audiences later used the word Frankenstein for both the monster and his maker.

The book uses other words for him. In the 1818 text on Project Gutenberg, by our count, "creature" appears 44 times, "fiend" 33, "monster" 30, "wretch" 27 and "dæmon" 19. Not every use refers to him; Victor and other characters call themselves wretches, too.

## How is the book different from the movies?

The NLM traces the change. In the novel, the creature is "a sensitive, reasoning, and articulate being whose crimes resulted from his mistreatment at the hands of humanity." On stage and screen he "mutated into a grunting brute."

| | Shelley's novel (1818) | Stage and film versions |
|---|---|---|
| The creature | Talks, reasons and teaches himself to read | A speechless or grunting killer, starting with the 1823 London play *Presumption; or, The Fate of Frankenstein* |
| His name | None | Often just "Frankenstein" |
| Why he kills | Abandoned by his maker and rejected by people | In Universal's 1931 film, he has been given a criminal's brain |
| How he moves | Fast and agile; Victor sees him cross a glacier "with superhuman speed" | Stiff, machine-like steps; Boris Karloff wore a five-pound metal rod next to his spine |

Thomas Edison's film company made the first film version in 1910, the NLM says. Shelley saw *Presumption* on Aug. 29, 1823, and wrote that "the play bill amused me extremely," because the cast list named the creature only with a blank.

## Where can I read Frankenstein for free?

Project Gutenberg lists the novel as public domain in the United States and offers two texts: the [1818 first edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/41445) (eBook 41445) and the 1831 revised edition (eBook 42324). The 1818 text opens with four letters from the explorer Robert Walton to his sister, Mrs. Saville, before Victor tells his story. Modern editions with new introductions, notes or illustrations can have their own copyright, so check before copying from one.

Draw your own creature rather than copying a film's makeup.
