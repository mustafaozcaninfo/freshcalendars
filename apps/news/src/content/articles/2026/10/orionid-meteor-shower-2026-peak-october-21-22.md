**The Orionid meteor shower, made of dust from Halley's Comet, peaks the night of Wednesday, Oct. 21 into Thursday, Oct. 22, 2026.** Under a dark sky you may see up to about 20 meteors an hour. This year the catch is the moon: it is a bright waxing gibbous that stays up until the small hours, so going out at midnight shows only the brightest meteors. The table below gives the moonset and the end of full darkness for 12 U.S. cities, from U.S. Naval Observatory data, so you know exactly when to set the alarm.

## How the night unfolds

| Local time | What you will see |
|---|---|
| Evening to midnight | The moon is high and bright. Orion rises in the east late in the evening; only an occasional bright meteor shows. |
| Midnight to moonset | The moon sinks in the west. More meteors appear as the radiant climbs, but moonlight still hides the faint ones. |
| Moonset to "dark until" | The best window: no moon, radiant high in the southeast to south, the most meteors of the night. |
| Twilight to sunrise | The sky brightens from the east and the count drops quickly. |

Meteor showers are usually strongest before dawn, because that side of Earth faces forward along its orbit and sweeps up more debris. This year that natural peak and the dark sky after moonset line up.

The Orionids have a broad maximum, so the mornings of Oct. 21, 22 and 23 can all produce similar rates. If clouds cover the peak, try the morning before or after; moonset comes about an hour earlier on Oct. 21 and about an hour later on Oct. 23.

## Where to look

The meteors seem to come from a point near the raised club of the constellation Orion, north of the reddish star Betelgeuse, which is high in the southeast to south before dawn. You do not need to stare at that point. Face southeast to south and look about halfway up the sky, 30 to 40 degrees away from Orion, where meteors show longer trails. Orionids are fast and often leave a glowing trail, called a train, that lingers for a second or two, and bright fireballs are possible.

## How to watch: a short checklist

1. **Check the sky.** Clouds matter more than anything else. If your forecast is cloudy for the peak, try Oct. 21 or Oct. 23.
2. **Get away from lights.** A park, a beach or the edge of town with a wide view of the southeastern to southern sky beats a backyard under a streetlight.
3. **Set an alarm for moonset.** Use the table above.
4. **Let your eyes adjust.** Give yourself 20 to 30 minutes in the dark without looking at a phone. If you need light, use a red flashlight or your phone's red-light or night mode with the brightness down.
5. **Get comfortable.** Lie back on a blanket, sleeping pad or reclining chair so you can take in a wide sweep of sky. Late October mornings are cold across much of the country; bring layers, a hat and a hot drink.
6. **Skip the telescope.** Binoculars and telescopes narrow your view; meteors are a naked-eye sight.
7. **Count in blocks.** Count for 15 minutes at a time; it keeps you looking up and shows how the rate climbs after moonset.

## Where they come from

Halley's Comet sheds ice and dust each time it passes the sun. Earth crosses that stream of debris twice a year: in October, producing the Orionids, and in early May, producing the Eta Aquariids. Halley itself was last near the sun in 1986 and is expected back in 2061, so these meteors are the easiest way to see a piece of it before then.
