**Mars comes within about 1.2° of Jupiter in the morning sky on Monday, Nov. 16, and Uranus reaches opposition on Wednesday, Nov. 25; a thin crescent Moon passed about 1° from Venus on Saturday, Nov. 7**, according to our night sky calendar computed from NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) Horizons data. NASA published its "What's Up" guide for November 2026 on Nov. 2; we calculated the month's planet events ourselves and took the Moon phases from the U.S. Naval Observatory (USNO).

Clocks fell back at 2 a.m. on Nov. 1, so every time below is Eastern Standard Time (EST) unless marked; see [when daylight saving time ends](/when-is/daylight-savings/).

## Which planets are in the morning sky?

Venus, Jupiter and Mars all rise before the Sun in November, according to JPL Horizons.

| Planet | Distance from the Sun in the sky | Brightness (magnitude) |
|---|---|---|
| Venus | 13° on Nov. 1, 30° on Nov. 15, 40° on Nov. 30 | About -4.2 on Nov. 1 to -4.9 at month's end |
| Jupiter | 74° on Nov. 1, 87° on Nov. 15, 101° on Nov. 30 | About -2.0 to -2.2 |
| Mars | 80° on Nov. 1, 87° on Nov. 15, 96° on Nov. 30 | About +0.8 to +0.4 |

Lower magnitudes are brighter. Venus is the brightest of the three and climbs higher before sunrise every day of the month, from only 13° from the Sun on Nov. 1 to 40° by Nov. 30. Venus stayed fairly low on Nov. 7 and keeps climbing, so find an open view to the east-southeast.

Jupiter and Mars are the other pair to find. The Moon, a day past last quarter, was near both in the east-southeast before dawn on Nov. 2 and again on Nov. 3. On Nov. 16 the planets themselves are about 1.2° apart, with Jupiter at magnitude -2.1 and Mars at +0.7; NASA's guide lists the pair close together on the mornings of Nov. 15 and 16, in the southeast before sunrise. On Nov. 30 the waning gibbous Moon returns to the same part of the sky, about 1.1° from Jupiter and 3.0° from Mars.

## Can I see Mercury, Saturn and Neptune?

Mercury passed between Earth and the Sun on Nov. 4 and was lost in the glare. It moves into the morning sky after about Nov. 10 and reaches its greatest western elongation, 19.6° from the Sun, at about 7 p.m. on Nov. 20. The best view is before dawn on Nov. 20-22, at about magnitude -0.1 to -0.7 in late November. Our guide to [Mercury retrograde](/news/mercury-retrograde-2026-ends-november-13-dates-times-2027/) lists every date and time through 2027.

Saturn is in the evening sky, 151° from the Sun on Nov. 1 and 120° by Nov. 30, at magnitude +0.5 to +0.6. It is best in the early evening in the south. Neptune is also an evening planet but shines at only magnitude 7.7, so it needs binoculars or a telescope; the Moon passes about 4.4° from it on Nov. 20.

## What is Uranus at opposition?

Opposition means Earth sits between the Sun and Uranus, so the planet is up all night. Uranus reaches it at about 6 p.m. EST on Wednesday, Nov. 25 (about 23:00 UTC), when it is 179.85° from the Sun in the sky. It is in Taurus near the Pleiades, at magnitude 5.6.

That brightness is at the limit of naked-eye visibility from very dark sites; binoculars from dark skies are the realistic tool. The timing is awkward: the Beaver Moon is full on Nov. 24 and still nearly full on Nov. 25, so its glare washes out faint objects. ## What else is in the sky this month?

The Southern Taurids peak on the night of Nov. 4-5, the Northern Taurids on Nov. 11-12 and the Leonids on Nov. 16-17, according to the American Meteor Society; NASA's November guide says the Southern Taurids peak around Nov. 5 and the Northern Taurids around Nov. 12, with the Leonids in the early mornings of Nov. 17 and 18 after the Moon sets. The details are in our [November 2026 meteor shower guide](/news/november-2026-meteor-showers-taurids-leonids/).

Before dawn on Tue., Nov. 24, the nearly full Moon passes in front of stars of the Pleiades as seen from the western United States, by our calculation with the open-source [Astronomy Engine](https://github.com/cosinekitty/astronomy). In Denver, Maia is hidden from about 5:22 to 6:04 a.m. Mountain Standard Time (MST); in Salt Lake City from 5:21 to 6:01 a.m. MST; in Seattle from 4:05 to 4:55 a.m. Pacific Standard Time (PST). Los Angeles sees only the fainter Taygeta, from 4:14 to 4:52 a.m. PST. From the East the Moon has set or the sky is light. Binoculars help next to such a bright Moon. ## Where do these times come from?

NASA's skywatching series, "What's Up," posted its November 2026 edition on Nov. 2, 2026, after we had computed these events on Oct. 28; we did not copy it. Our times come from the [NASA JPL Horizons](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/) ephemeris (a geocentric calculation at hourly steps), converted from UTC to Eastern time. Moon phases are from the [USNO Astronomical Applications moon phases data](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/api/moon/phases/year?year=2026). Times are rounded to the hour for planet events, so an event can fall within an hour of the time shown.
