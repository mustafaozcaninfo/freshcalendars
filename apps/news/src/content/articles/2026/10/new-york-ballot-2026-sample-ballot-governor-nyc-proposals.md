**New Yorkers voted Tuesday, Nov. 3, 2026, for governor, attorney general and comptroller, and for every seat in the U.S. House and the state Legislature.** Gov. Kathy Hochul won a second full term over Bruce Blakeman, and Attorney General Letitia James and Comptroller Thomas DiNapoli were re-elected, according to Gothamist, ABC7 New York and FOX 5 New York. There was no U.S. Senate race. New York City voters also passed all five city charter questions in unofficial results. Below: every statewide candidate with party lines and result, the city questions in plain terms, and the dates that mattered.

## Statewide races

| Office | Candidate | Party lines | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governor and lieutenant governor | Kathy Hochul and Adrienne Adams | Democratic, Working Families | Won |
| Governor and lieutenant governor | Bruce Blakeman and Todd Hood | Republican, Conservative, Vote Affordable | Did not win; conceded |
| Attorney general | Letitia James | Democratic, Working Families | Won |
| Attorney general | Saritha Komatireddy | Republican, Conservative | Did not win |
| Comptroller | Thomas DiNapoli | Democratic, Working Families | Won |
| Comptroller | Joseph Hernandez | Republican, Conservative | Did not win |

Governor and lieutenant governor run as one ticket, so voters cast one vote for the pair. Results are as reported on election night and the next day, before official certification. New York allows fusion voting: a candidate can be nominated by more than one party and appears on each line, and votes on all of a candidate's lines are added together. The table lists every candidate the state certified for these three offices. Which of the other races a voter saw depended on where they lived.

## Every seat in Congress and the Legislature

All 26 of New York's U.S. House seats are up, along with all 63 state Senate seats and all 150 Assembly seats; every member serves a two-year term. State Supreme Court justice races, the state's main trial court, are on the ballot in 11 judicial districts. Which of these races a voter saw depended on where they lived.

## New York City's five charter questions

The Commission on Government Efficiency, the city's 2026 charter revision commission, released five proposals on July 23, and the NYC Board of Elections put them on the ballot as questions. Each one changes the City Charter now that voters approved it. The summaries below follow the board's published wording.

| Question | What it does | A "yes" vote | A "no" vote | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Public space | Faster approval to use sidewalks and streets for ramps, planters and cafes | Removes mandatory hearings and Council disapproval of sidewalk cafes, keeping community input | Keeps the longer process with a public hearing and Council review | Passed |
| 2. Contracting | Less paperwork for city contractors; Procurement Policy Board meets quarterly | Lets the board simplify the contractor questionnaire and the mayor delegate some contract approvals | Leaves the current rules and longer questionnaire | Passed |
| 3. Streets and property | Faster street safety projects, sales of unused city property and office leases | Simplifies street project approval, property sales and office leasing | Keeps current consultation and Council approvals | Passed |
| 4. Building permits | One central hub for construction permits; waterfront permits move to the Department of Buildings | Creates the hub and lets more Buildings officials issue approvals | Leaves the laws unchanged | Passed |
| 5. Rainy day fund | A target for money held in reserve | Requires the mayor to set a formula for deposits, subject to the city budget | Leaves the laws unchanged, with no target | Passed |

City & State and The Riverdale Press report that all five passed in unofficial results from the NYC Board of Elections; The Riverdale Press figures show each with more than 61% yes. The commission says the changes would, for example, cut the outdoor dining application from about eight months to two and speed up street safety projects such as bike lanes by at least a third; those are its estimates. The reserve target it proposed is 12% of the city's tax revenue. Outside the city, check your county board of elections for any local questions. No statewide ballot proposals were on the 2026 ballot.

## How to see a sample ballot at the next election

- **New York City:** enter your address on the NYC Board of Elections' "Find Your Poll Site" page at vote.nyc. It shows a ballot specific to your district, along with your poll site.
- **Elsewhere in the state:** your county board of elections posts sample ballots. The state's voter lookup at voterlookup.elections.ny.gov shows your registration, your poll site and the status of a mail ballot.
- **Other states:** our [sample ballot by ZIP code guide](/news/sample-ballot-by-zip-code-2026-texas-ballot/) lists the official lookup for every state.

## Key dates and rules

| Date | What happened |
|---|---|
| Sat., Oct. 24 | Registration deadline (applications had to be received); early voting opened; last day to apply for a mail ballot online or by mail |
| Oct. 24-Nov. 1 | Early in-person voting; hours were set by each county |
| Mon., Nov. 2 | Last day to apply for a mail ballot in person at your county board |
| Tue., Nov. 3 | Election Day, polls open 6 a.m. to 9 p.m.; mail ballots had to be postmarked by that day |

New York voters did not need to show ID unless they did not provide identification when they registered; first-time voters had to give it on or with their registration form.
